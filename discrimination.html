8  Discrimination

The previous chapter was devoted to wage differences under perfect competition in the labour market. Here we discuss wage differences under imperfect competition, focusing on discrimination.

8.1 Discrimination and its dimensions

8.1.1 Defining discrimination

Labour market discrimination occurs when workers in one group receive less favourable treatment than workers in another group despite being equally productive. Relevant factors include ability, education, training, and experience. Discrimination can affect hiring, access to particular occupations, promotion, pay, and working conditions.

In the case of wages, the question is whether equally productive workers receive different pay solely because they belong to different groups.

8.1.2 Types of discrimination

We can distinguish two types of discrimination according to when they occur.

  • Pre-market discrimination occurs before people enter the labour market. It can affect how they acquire human capital. For example, members of one group may find it harder to gain access to education or vocational training because of their group membership.

  • Labour market discrimination occurs after people enter the labour market and begin offering their labour. It can take at least three forms.

    • Wage discrimination
    • Discrimination in employment and unemployment
    • Occupational discrimination or segregation

How does each form of labour market discrimination affect workers?

Forms of labour market discrimination

  • Wage discrimination. Workers in one group earn less than workers in another group for doing the same work.

  • Discrimination in employment and unemployment. Workers in one group may be less likely to find a job or more likely to lose their jobs and become unemployed. As a result, unemployment is higher in that group.

  • Occupational discrimination or segregation. Workers may be excluded from particular occupations because of their group membership. Members of one group may therefore be excluded from some occupations and overrepresented in others, even when differences in human capital or other relevant factors cannot explain the pattern.

8.1.3 Theories of labour market discrimination

Having defined discrimination and distinguished its main forms, we can now ask why it occurs.

Economists have proposed several models to explain labour market discrimination. We focus on three of them.

  • The taste-based discrimination model
  • The statistical discrimination model
  • The occupational crowding model

8.2 The taste-based discrimination model

In the chapter on labour demand, we studied how an employer chooses how much labour to hire to maximise profit. The taste-based discrimination model introduces an additional consideration into that decision. The employer has a personal preference about which groups of workers to employ and is willing to forgo some profit to act on that preference.

Specifically, the model assumes that the employer prefers to maintain physical and social distance from certain groups of workers and is willing to bear a cost to do so.
